Bevel manual

Six-band parametric EQ with a real-time analyzer. Bevel shapes tonal balance without adding a sound of its own: what you hear is the move you made and nothing else.

Warren Labs · Trueness line · VST3 · AU · Standalone · macOS

What it's for

Everyday corrective and tonal EQ: clearing mud, taming a harsh band, adding air, rolling off rumble. Six bands cover almost every job, and every band can be any of six filter shapes.

Bevel is minimum-phase: no pre-ringing and no latency. A bell or shelf at 0 dB is not "nearly" flat; it is bypassed exactly. With every band at 0 dB, as it opens, Bevel's output is bit-for-bit identical to its input.

Working on the plot

There are no knobs. Every band is a numbered dot on the plot, and you edit it directly:

ActionDoes
Drag a dotFrequency (left–right) and gain (up–down) together
Mouse wheel over a dotQ, the band's width. Up = narrower
Double-click a dotSwitches that band on or off. An off band is a hollow ring
Right-click a dotChoose its filter type

A hint along the bottom of the plot repeats this until you grab a dot.

All six start switched on at 0 dB, which is the same as off.

Filter types (right-click)

  • Bell: boost or cut around the frequency. Q sets the width.
  • Low Shelf / High Shelf: boost or cut everything below / above the frequency.
  • High Pass / Low Pass: remove everything below / above the frequency, at 12 dB per octave. Q sets the resonance at the corner: 0.71 is the smooth default, higher values add a bump.
  • Notch: a deep, narrow cut for a single problem frequency. Q sets the width.

High Pass, Low Pass and Notch have no gain. Dragging their dot up or down moves the dot but does not change the sound. The amber curve always shows what is actually being applied.

Ranges

Frequency 20 Hz–20 kHz · Gain ±18 dB · Q 0.1–10.

Power

Bypass, with a click-free crossfade.

The display

  • Amber curve: the combined response of all six bands, the EQ actually being applied.
  • Dim spectrum behind it: a live analyzer of the signal passing through.
  • Dots: one per band. Filled when on, hollow when off, outlined when selected.

A worked example: a boomy acoustic guitar

  1. Band 1: right-click → High Pass, drag it to about 80 Hz. The low rumble and mic handling noise go; the guitar itself shouldn't change.
  2. Band 2: find the boom. Drag it up about 8 dB and sweep it left and right between 150 and 400 Hz. Where it sounds worst, stop.
  3. Now drag it down to about −4 dB at that frequency. Wheel up for a narrower Q if the cut takes body with it.
  4. Band 5: drag up 2 dB around 5–6 kHz for pick definition, if the part needs it.
  5. Toggle Power. Is the guitar clearer, or just quieter? Match levels before you decide. Cuts make things quieter, and quieter always sounds worse.

The boost-and-sweep trick in step 2 is the fastest way to find a problem frequency. Just remember to turn it into a cut.

Tips

  • Cut narrow, boost wide. Problems are usually narrow; flattering moves are usually broad.
  • Double-click a band to hear it off and on without losing its setting.
  • Bevel has no output gain. If a big boost pushes your level up, pull the fader or the next plugin down.
