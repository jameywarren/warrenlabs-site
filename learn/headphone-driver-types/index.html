Driver types: dynamic, planar, and electrostatic

Short answer

The driver is the motor that moves air in a headphone. Dynamic is the common, efficient, easy-to-drive type; planar magnetic is fast with tight bass but heavier and power-hungry; electrostatic is effortless and ultra-low-distortion but needs a special energizer and deep pockets. Dynamic — the default, and it’s good

A dynamic driver is a voice coil attached to a cone or dome that moves in a magnetic field — a miniature loudspeaker. It powers everything from earbuds to flagships because it is efficient (easy to drive off a phone), robust, and can sound superb. Its reputation for “punch” and warmth is a tendency, not a rule; plenty of dynamics are ruler-neutral. Most of the Archiveis dynamic.

Planar magnetic — fast and tight, but hungry

A planar driver stretches a very thin, flat diaphragm between arrays of magnets, so the whole surface moves as one. The payoff is speed, low distortion, and tight, even bass. The costs are weight and appetite: most planars are relatively insensitive and want a real amp. The Audeze LCD line is the classic example.

Electrostatic — effortless, and expensive

An electrostatic driver suspends an ultra-thin charged film between two perforated plates and moves it with high voltage — supplied by a dedicated energizer, not an ordinary amp. The result is a delicate, effortless, extremely low-distortion sound that many consider the pinnacle. The catch is cost, rarity, and the matched energizer. The Sennheiser Orpheus is the legend here.

A word on IEM drivers

In-ears add their own vocabulary: tiny balanced-armature drivers (efficient, often stacked several to a shell) and dynamic drivers, sometimes combined. The same rule holds — the driver mix shapes tendencies, but a well-tuned single dynamic can beat a badly-tuned multi-driver.

The through-line: driver technology sets a headphone’s character, but tuning — its frequency balance — is what you notice first and what you can actually change. That is the partAttune lets you tailor, whatever the driver behind it.

The honest bottom line

Learn the tendencies — dynamic efficiency, planar control, electrostatic delicacy — but don’t shop by driver type. A great tuning on a humble dynamic beats a mediocre tuning on an exotic driver. The technology is the character; the tuning is the sound.

Common questions

What is the difference between dynamic and planar headphones?

A dynamic driver uses a voice coil and a cone or dome, like a tiny speaker — efficient and easy to drive. A planar magnetic driver uses a thin flat diaphragm between magnets — fast, low-distortion, with tight bass, but heavier and usually power-hungry.

Are planar magnetic headphones better?

Not automatically. Planars tend toward tight, controlled bass and low distortion, but a well-tuned dynamic beats a poorly-tuned planar. The driver sets tendencies; the tuning decides whether you enjoy it.

What is an electrostatic headphone?

One that uses an ultra-thin charged film between two stators, driven by high voltage from a dedicated energizer — not a normal headphone amp. They are prized for effortless detail and very low distortion, but they are expensive and rare.

Do planar magnetic headphones need an amp?

Usually yes. Most planars are relatively insensitive and want clean power to sound their best. Check impedance and sensitivity together to know how hard a specific one is to drive.

Which driver type is best?

None, universally. Each has tendencies — dynamic punch and efficiency, planar speed and bass control, electrostatic delicacy — but tuning matters more than the driver. Choose by the sound and the practicalities, not the technology label.
